1 Mission Context, Objectives, and Success Criteria

1.1 Executive Summary

Project ANTARA is a 3U CubeSat mission at BITS Pilani, K.K. Birla Goa Campus, dedicated to
the in-situ characterization of the South Atlantic Anomaly (SAA) radiation environment. Op-
erating in a 600 km Sun-Synchronous Orbit (97.6° inclination), the spacecraft carries a custom
Flux Particle Detector Unit (FPDU) payload to measure unidirectional differential proton flux
across the 5–100 MeV range in a proposed multi-channel energy reconstruction.
The mission is structured around three tiers of success that scale spatial resolution, coverage,
and scientific ambition. Beyond its scientific objective, ANTARA serves as a student satellite
training platform, exposing undergraduate students to the complete spacecraft development
lifecycle from concept through operations.
This document represents the Mission Concept Review (MCR) package for Project ANTARA,
consolidating mission requirements, architecture, orbit design, operational concept, subsys-
tem design status, and simulation results.

1.2 Mission Need

The ANTARA mission has been conceptualized to address a combination of scientific, edu-
cational, and national-level needs associated with the growing importance of space-based
radiation studies and indigenous small-satellite development. Modern spacecraft operating
in Low Earth Orbit (LEO) are increasingly exposed to harsh radiation environments that can
significantly affect mission reliability, onboard electronics, detector systems, and long-term
spacecraft survivability. Among these environments, the South Atlantic Anomaly (SAA) rep-
resents one of the most severe and scientifically significant radiation regions encountered by
satellites in LEO.

Although several trapped-radiation models such as AP8 and AP9 currently exist, substantial
uncertainties remain regarding temporal proton-flux variability, storm-time behavior, direc-
tional anisotropies, and localized radiation structures within the SAA. Existing models are pri-
marily statistical and often lack sufficient real-time data required for modern small-satellite
missions and future radiation-aware spacecraft operations.
The ANTARA mission seeks to contribute toward closing these gaps through direct in-situ
measurement of high-energy proton populations using a dedicated CubeSat operating in a
Sun-Synchronous Orbit. The project also enables students to gain practical experience in
CubeSat designing and modelling.

1.2.1 Educational Motive

One of the primary motivations behind ANTARA is the creation of an educational and re-
search platform capable of providing hands-on experience in real-world spacecraft develop-
ment.
The mission enables participating students to experience all major phases of aerospace
project development. Unlike isolated academic laboratory experiments, CubeSat missions
introduce students to highly constrained engineering environments where mass, power,
thermal limits and others interact simultaneously. This systems-level complexity is one of
the most important educational benefits of the ANTARA mission.

1.2.2 Scientific Motive

The primary scientific motivation behind ANTARA is the need for improved understanding
of the trapped proton environment within the South Atlantic Anomaly and its implications
for spacecraft operations, radiation modelling, and near-Earth space science.
The South Atlantic Anomaly represents the region where the inner Van Allen radiation belt
approaches closest to Earth due to the offset and tilt of Earth’s magnetic dipole. Spacecraft
traversing this region are exposed to significantly elevated fluxes of energetic charged parti-
cles, particularly high-energy protons capable of producing Single Event Upsets (SEUs), sen-
sor noise, detector saturation, memory corruption, semiconductor degradation, and cumula-
tive radiation damage.
ANTARA aims to contribute toward this scientific need by directly measuring Unidirectional
Differential Proton Flux (FPDU) within the energy range of 5 MeV to 100 MeV using an on-
board proton detection payload. The mission seeks to generate spatially and temporally cor-
related radiation datasets that can be compared against existing radiation models including
AP8 and AP9.

1.2.3 National Motive

From a national perspective, ANTARA aligns strongly with India’s growing emphasis on in-
digenous space technology development, scientific self-reliance, and advanced research capa-
bility. The mission contributes toward India’s requirement for indigenous expertise in space-
craft systems engineering, radiation-aware electronics, mission operations, and advanced
satellite technologies.
Also, the satellites in the past few years have faced various anomalies with this region due to
which communication signals with them were lost. Data collected by ANTARA maps the shift-
ing borders of the SAA, allowing Indian satellites to shield or power down domestic defense
and communication satellites before they sustain damage. ANTARA provides an opportunity

1.3 Mission Significance

1.3.1 Primary Flight Objective

The primary objective is to measure and map unidirectional differential proton flux in the
South Atlantic Anomaly over the 5–100 MeV range.

1.3.2 Secondary Mission Objectives

The secondary objectives are to examine East–West asymmetry, characterize the bifurcation
of the SAA, investigate atmospheric-loss coupling, and develop corrections to radiation-model
predictions from ANTARA measurements. Storm-time comparisons remain an exploratory
objective dependent on available observations. The detector architecture and channel def-
inition remain under development; the science-success criteria are proposed targets to be
tested against actual detection and sampling capability.

2 Mission Overview and Orbit Design
The ANTARA mission has been conceived as a dedicated scientific CubeSat mission focused
on characterizing the high-energy proton radiation environment within the South Atlantic
Anomaly (SAA). The spacecraft is intended to operate in a near-circular Sun-Synchronous
Low Earth Orbit. The detailed orbit propagation and trade-study results are provided in Ap-
pendix A.6 and Appendix B.

2.1 Spacecraft Overview

ANTARA is a 3U CubeSat with an on-board computer, an electrical power system, an atti-
tude determination and control system, a communications system, and the scientific payload.
The initial orbit is approximately 600 km at an inclination of 97.6∘ . No propulsion system is
planned.
The 3U spacecraft dimensions are approximately 100 × 100 × 340.5 mm. The mass budget and
3 kg design limit are assessed in Section 5.3.

2.2 Orbit Selection

The preliminary orbit baseline is a 600 km Sun-Synchronous Orbit with an inclination of
97.6∘ and an eccentricity of approximately 0.001. The latest seven-case RAAN power study
reports its largest modeled mean generation at 180∘ ; power and battery behavior depend on
the insertion RAAN. ANTARA does not control the launch RAAN, so the spacecraft is being
assessed across multiple RAAN cases. The approved future analysis epoch is 1 January 2029;
the evaluated studies retain the source epochs and assumptions identified in Section 4.

2.3 Mission Lifecycle

The mission is divided into Launch, Deployment, Cold and Dark, Detumbling, Commissioning,
Nominal Operations, Extended Operations, and Mission End phases. Following deployment
the spacecraft activates after the planned delay, enters Cold and Dark, performs detumbling,
and proceeds toward power-positive operations and commissioning. Nominal operations
consist of Sun Pointing, Detection, and Relay, with Safe Mode for recovery.
The extended phase begins after nominal mission objectives have been met. The minimum,
full, and extended mission duration targets are defined in Section 1.4. The detailed phase
descriptions and mode transitions are retained in Appendices B and A.1.

3.3 Scientific Payload

The current FPDU concept uses five 6×6×14 mm main scintillator blocks in series along X, plus
one 6 × 6 × 2 mm front scintillator. Particles enter the +𝑋 face and travel inward toward −𝑋.
Main-block SiPMs attach to ±𝑌 (ten sensors), with one further front SiPM: eleven total. Teflon
coats ±𝑍 at 0.11 mm per face, giving 6.22 mm coated Z dimension. EJ-550 grease provides
optical coupling. These dimensions define the current design; material selection, inter-block
optical isolation and packaged CAD remain to be established. The historical silicon entrance
is removed; a rear scintillator is optional and unselected. The proton-energy requirement
remains 5–100 MeV.
The proposed chain is scintillator, SiPM, ASIC, ADC, MCU or FPGA, classification, reconstruc-
tion, 64-bin histogram, and storage/downlink. ASIC shaping is 20 ns. Coincidence-window
references are 200 ns nominal, at least 250 ns including allowance, and approximately 300 ns
preferred. A separate stated 20 ns post-shaping interval has unresolved meaning. End-to-end
dead time includes ADC and processing latency, which are not yet allocated. MCU is preferred
only if the entire architecture meets timing, throughput, buffering and power needs; no pro-
cessor is selected.
The incident-rate study predicts proton maxima of 358.920 Hz (mean map) and 975.659 Hz
(99th-percentile map), using isotropic conversion, 𝐺 = 1 cm2 sr, unit efficiency and an in-

2029 planning. They provide a spatial rate basis for electronics sizing; electron incidence
must also be included. The assumed geometric factor and efficiency are not demonstrated
detector acceptance, and the calculation does not model shielding or energy reconstruction.

3.4 Attitude Determination and Control System (ADCS)

ing SAA operations, +Y relay pointing toward the ground station, and Safe Mode recovery.
The applicable pointing and actuator-authority envelopes depend on the selected unit and
payload/antenna acceptance. Mode transitions are given in Appendix A.1.
The supplied historical detumble summary contains 50 000 trials, with 16 133 reported suc-
cesses (32.266%). Successful trials alone have median completion 951.5 s and 95th percentile

initial accelerations, which the current source rejects. This synthetic campaign is incompat-

integrated scenario separately completes a 2460 s detumble event and proceeds to scheduled

Scientific feasibility — CONDITIONALLY SUPPORTED. The 5–100 MeV objective, spatial

A.1.3 Supervisory Priority and Safety Overrides

The supervisor evaluates spacecraft survival before granting any scheduled activity. The pro-
posed policy separates a task that is temporarily ineligible from a condition requiring Safe
mode. For example, an unavailable contact or unsettled pointing defers Relay or Science; a
critical battery, thermal or control-state violation preempts the active task. The corrected
annual run contains Safe-mode occupancy, but this alone does not establish which physical
fault caused each entry or qualify the recovery guards.

Priority when opportunities overlap. Survival and recovery take precedence over science
and downlink. Among otherwise admissible tasks, this planning interpretation uses the pre-
dicted consequences of deferral: loss of a scarce science window, queue overflow before the
next contact, and the energy needed to execute and recover from a slew. Relay is not assumed
universally superior to Science, nor is simultaneous acquisition and downlink credited. A de-
terministic scheduler must record the selected task and reason for deferral. Numerical prior-
ities, queue watermarks and energy reserves remain to be allocated; the corrected exposure
and backlog results constrain that trade but do not identify an optimal flight scheduler.

Interruption, safe state and recovery. On a critical override, the proposed response
closes the valid acquisition interval, marks any uncertain data, inhibits nonessential
payload/transmitter loads and preserves recoverable queue and sequence state. Health
monitoring and feasible attitude control continue within the surviving power budget. Safe
mode is therefore a fault-dependent operating state, not a promise of uninterrupted Sun
pointing: excessive rates may first require Detumble, while a sensor fault may prevent the
nominal attitude solution.
Recovery requires evidence that the initiating condition has cleared and that the intended
next mode is admissible. Separate entry and recovery guards, with persistence checks where
appropriate, are proposed to prevent repeated mode switching near a threshold. Their val-
ues are not inferred from sampling cadence. Recovery proceeds through Detumble if rate
stabilization is needed, otherwise through Sun Pointing and readiness checks before science
or relay resumes. Interrupted transfers require integrity and sequence reconciliation be-
fore records are retired from persistent storage; the final acknowledgement/retry protocol

A.4.1 Thermal Model and Spacecraft Geometry

tance. These values form a preliminary thermal representation, not measured properties of
the final assembly.

Downlink Architecture
The baseline downlink rate is 15–18 kbps (historical, superseded by the ideal 19.2 kbps
MCR assumption).
The scientific payload does not transmit individual proton events. Instead, the onboard FPGA
accumulates events into 64-bin spatial-energy histograms. Each histogram contains:
64×4=256 bytes
At one update per minute, the science data rate is approximately:
(256×8)/60≈34 bps
This corresponds to an estimated 300–400 KB/day (historical estimate, superseded by
the v3 sensitivity analysis) of science data.
With two daily contacts totalling approximately 780 s, a 15 kbps link provides:
15,000×780≈11.7 Mbit
Thus, the available capacity provides approximately a 4× margin over the expected science-
data volume, allowing for housekeeping, operational logs, attitude data, protocol overhead,
and retransmissions.
The 9.6 kbps value used in earlier analysis is superseded by the architecture-driven 15–18
kbps (historical, superseded by the ideal 19.2 kbps MCR assumption) baseline.

Control Modes and Algorithms
Detumbling. Following deployment, the ADCS executes a two-stage control law: an initial
B-dot magnetic detumble to reduce high angular rates acquired during deployment, followed
by reaction-wheel damping to bring the spacecraft to a stable, low-rate condition suitable for
Sun Pointing Mode entry.
Simulation results show detumble completing in approximately 2,460 seconds from initial
body rates of [6.0, −5.5, 4.5] °/s. No hard detumble timeout is currently defined.
Sun Pointing. The ADCS orients the spacecraft +Z face toward the Sun to maximize solar-
array power generation, using reaction wheels for fine pointing and magnetorquers for mo-
mentum dumping.
Detection Mode Pointing. During SAA traversal, the ADCS aligns the +X axis (payload bore-
sight) with the spacecraft velocity vector to support directional proton flux measurement
and East-West asymmetry analysis. Simulation results show a mean attitude error of approx-
imately 2°, against a threshold of ≤5° and a goal of ≤2°.
Relay Mode Pointing. The ADCS points the +Y face (directional relay antenna) toward the
ground station to optimize downlink signal strength during scheduled contacts.
Slewing. Mode transitions between Sun Pointing, Detection, and Relay require dedicated
slew maneuvers. The 24-hour DITL simulation recorded 18 slew events with slew angles
ranging from 5.4° to 175.8°; large-angle slews were identified as consuming non-negligible
time and should be factored into mission scheduling and duty-cycle analysis.
Saturation Handling.          Reaction wheel momentum saturation is managed through
magnetorquer-based momentum unloading. If saturation persists beyond a defined hold
timeout and pointing diverges, the FSM commands a transition to Safe Mode as a fault-
protection response, this is the second-highest-priority transition in the mode state machine
after critical battery conditions

C.1.5 On-Board Computer (OBC)

The On-Board Computer is the central computational and operational-control unit of the AN-
TARA spacecraft. The COTS model we have taken for the simulation is GomSpace Nanomind
A3200.
Purpose
The OBC serves as the spacecraft’s primary operational-management system and continu-
ously monitors spacecraft status while coordinating all major mission activities.
At 3.3V bus channel, The OBC can run on various clock speeds from 8 - 32MHz depending on
the state of the CubeSat with an average power consumption of 0.17W
Functional Responsibilities
The ANTARA OBC performs mission sequencing, state monitoring, command execution,
health monitoring, payload control, data-storage management, and communications
scheduling. Payload control is closely integrated with orbit propagation, ADCS orientation,
SAA traversal prediction, and spacecraft power availability, enabling synchronised science
operations during South Atlantic Anomaly crossings.
Flight Software Philosophy
ANTARA uses a state-machine-based flight software architecture designed to maximise re-
liability, deterministic behaviour, operational predictability, fault containment, and verifi-
cation simplicity. Each operational mode corresponds to a defined spacecraft state. State
transitions occur only when predefined operational conditions are satisfied, such as battery
thresholds, spacecraft attitude stability, payload readiness, and subsystem health. The state-
machine architecture simplifies mission simulation, operational verification, fault diagnosis,
and long-duration autonomous operations.

G.8 Datasheet alignment checks

The supplied ICEPS2 datasheet describes a 2S1P battery pack with nominal energy 28.8 Wh.
The 25 Wh value remains the mission simulation planning capacity. The supplied TensorTech
ADCS-10m datasheet explicitly describes a control-moment gyroscope configuration. Since
ANTARA intends to use a reaction-wheel and magnetorquer integrated system, that datasheet
cannot be treated as proof of the selected ADCS hardware configuration. A matching vendor
model and specification must be supplied. The GNSS, radio, antenna, deployment-panel and
structure datasheets are candidate mechanical/electrical references, pending interface selec-
tion.

G.9 Approved 2029 Planning Baseline

The mission reference is 1 January 2029: 3U, maximum 3.0 kg, approximately 600 km SSO,
97.6∘ inclination, eccentricity approximately 0.001, launch-dependent RAAN, 25 Wh battery
planning capacity and reaction wheels with magnetorquers. The team accepts the supplied
RAAN data for 2029 planning; the inspected local MCR runtime still starts in 2026, so its gener-
ating epoch remains unverified. This does not prevent use as a clearly identified preliminary
reference. The supplied radiation map retains its verified 2026 source epoch.
The approved FPDU has five longitudinal main blocks and one front scintillator, eleven SiPMs,
no silicon entrance and optional rear detection. Optical isolation, material, calibrated re-
sponse and processor selection remain open. Appendix A.2 controls current construction
and timing. Approximately five years is the operational objective; modeled decay to 300 km
and subsequent reentry are separate quantities. The 8–10-year cutoff study retains its config-
uration and censoring limitations (Appendix H).

H.4 Monte Carlo statistics: scope and interpretation

The provided mission_statistics(1).csv corresponds numerically to the 180∘ RAAN row
for average power (11.17485 W), mean SOC (0.98234), mean run-minimum SOC (0.73446), cy-
cle count (6565.43) and lifetime (8.58241 years). Its confidence intervals should not be in-
terpreted as 99% population coverage without verification of the statistical method and in-
dependent sample count; the raw study includes only two runs per RAAN. The power and
lifetime fields represent modeled quantities, not measured solar electrical output or a veri-
fied physical lifetime.

H.5 Design implication for the MCR

The operational science campaign targets a roughly five-year mission, while the newest drag
study points toward a potentially longer residence time before reaching 300 km. These state-
ments are not interchangeable. The MCR should maintain the five-year operational objective
while qualifying the uncertain passive decay and post-mission disposal timeline. Detailed
DITL operations are being rerun separately; their future results should be incorporated with
independent identification of date, simulation revision and model assumptions.
